[Interviewer]: Speaking of washerwomen... from your studio, sight and sound are constantly accompanied by their work along the pipes. How much of this popular world is present in your paintings?



[Ludovico Carracci]: More than the critics would like to admit! When we founded the Academy with my cousins Annibale and Agostino, we told ourselves a simple thing: enough with the fake, artificial figures, stiff as statues. We wanted true nature. And where do you find true nature? You find it in the strong arms of a washerwoman rubbing clothes on the stone, in the curve of her back bent by toil , in the way a damp cloth falls and creates real creases. Look at the skin of that woman over there working in the sun: that redness on her elbows, that naturalness... If I had to paint a Madonna or a Saint Martha, I would n't look for an ideal model: I'd look for the true dignity of the women of Porto.



[Interviewer]: Your name is inextricably linked to the church and the devotion to Saint Charles Borromeo, an austere and pivotal figure in the Counter-Reformation. How does great sacred painting fit into the earthly environment of this neighborhood?



[Ludovico Carracci]: Saint Charles Borromeo was not a saint to be hung on a pedestal: he was a man who went among pestilent people, who walked barefoot through the streets, who touched the suffering flesh of the people. Here, near the church of San Carlo, among the common people, faith is not a theoretical exercise. It is pure hope against hunger, against sudden floods of the canal, against epidemics. When I paint an altarpiece for these churches, I don't want the faithful to feel excluded. I want the boatman or the washerwoman to enter San Carlo, look at the painting and say: "I know that face, that pain is mine, that light speaks to me."



[Interviewer]: Your cousins, Annibale and Agostino, left for Rome to gain the great favor of the cardinals and popes. You, on the other hand, remained faithful to Bologna and this corner of the sea. Any regrets?



[Ludovico Carracci]: (Sighs fondly, looking toward the window) Hannibal is an immense genius; Rome was his destiny, and it was right that he should go and conquer the world. But my soul is here. Bologna has a style of painting that has no need of Roman grandeur: it has the drama of affection, the truth of shadows, the warmth of earth and brick. And then... who would prepare soup in Rome with this flavor? Who would give me this misty, humid light that blurs the outlines of my paintings, endowing them with that subtle poetry you only find when the fog rises from the canals at sunset?
