Anecdotes and historical curiosities about the Beccari

  • From the goat to the profession of "beccaro": In the dialect and ancient Italian of Bologna, the butcher was called beccaro (or beccaio). The term has no connection with birds, but derives from the Medieval Latin beccarius, which in turn is related to beccus, meaning "beak" or goat—one of the most common meats on medieval and Renaissance tables. It's no coincidence that the guild's coat of arms often featured an ox or goat.

  • The strict controls of the scales in the Quadrilatero: In the Middle Ages and the 17th century, the Beccari were not simple shopkeepers, but a wealthy and influential guild, with its own internal court and a body of overseers. Their headquarters throbbed in the heart of the Quadrilatero, between Via Caprarie and Via Drapperie, where at number 3 Via Caprarie the 15th-century stone coat of arms featuring an ox passing between two lions still stands. To prevent fraud regarding the weight or the sale of spoiled meat, the guild's stewards applied exemplary sanctions: anyone who falsified the scales had their goods immediately confiscated and was exposed to public ridicule in the market square.

  • A silent challenge to the noble families: Financing two consecutive arches along the Portico of San Luca represented a true affirmation of social prestige for the butchers. Along Via Saragozza, senatorial families vied to engrave their coats of arms on the pillars; by responding with a double lot (Arches 125 and 126), the Butchers demonstrated that artisanal work and the trade of their workshops had the same civic dignity and economic weight as the city's aristocracy.
