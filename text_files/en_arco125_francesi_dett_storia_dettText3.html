Curiosities and Period Anecdotes

1. Chaos Among the Butchers' Stalls Until 1796, no one could sell meat in the Quadrilatero without the authorization and mark of the Arte dei Beccai, which oversaw weights, prices, and hygiene. When the pro-French government abolished the guilds and proclaimed freedom of enterprise, a minor commercial earthquake broke out in the alleys: dozens of new improvised vendors set up stalls without paying any registration fee, sparking protests from the old master butchers, who accused them of selling inferior cuts and violating centuries-old trade rules.

2. "A Hat Without a Head and a Tree Without Roots" On the evening of October 18, 1796, just steps from the Quadrilatero, in the middle of Piazza Maggiore, Bolognese patriots erected a tall poplar as a Tree of Liberty, topped with a red Phrygian cap. While citizens and French soldiers danced joyfully around the trunk, the old nobles and conservatives tied to the former Papal government watched the scene from afar, murmuring a famous ironic motto arrived from Paris: "Un chapeau sans tête, un arbre sans racines" (a hat with no head inside and a cut tree with no roots, destined to wither quickly).

3. The Chiseling Away of Coats of Arms With the arrival of the French, a decree ordered the removal of all noble, papal, and guild emblems from palace facades and beneath the porticoes, in the name of republican equality. Many stone and terracotta plaques in the Quadrilatero were chiseled off or covered with lime: in their place appeared painted inscriptions bearing the mottos Liberty and Equality, while the new owners hurried to modernize the entrances and arches of their properties to erase every trace of the old regime.

4. The Misunderstanding Over the City Colors In the very first days of the French occupation, the elderly Bolognese senators hoped to restore their ancient medieval municipal autonomy and asked Napoleon for permission to have citizens wear a white and red cockade, the historic colors of Bologna. The French commissioners instead imposed the colors of the Revolution, which a few months later—in January 1797—evolved into the first green, white, and red Tricolore flag, designed by the Bolognese painter Mauro Gandolfi himself.
